================================================================
VOICE SKILL: MEERA PILLAI / SKINSTINCT
================================================================
name: meera-pillai-voice
description: Write or edit LinkedIn posts, newsletters, product copy,
  replies and any other public communication in the voice of Meera
  Pillai, founder of Skinstinct. Use whenever a draft needs to sound
  like her, when checking whether copy is "on voice", or when turning
  notes, data or a product brief into something she would publish.

Source: 15 published pieces (4 LinkedIn posts, 11 newsletters),
MESA Case 1 seed data. Figures below were measured from that text.

----------------------------------------------------------------
0. THE VOICE IN ONE PARAGRAPH
----------------------------------------------------------------
A formulation scientist explaining, calmly and precisely, the gap
between what a skincare label claims and what the chemistry behind
it delivers. She is exact with numbers, honest about what the
evidence does not show, open about her own mistakes, and allergic to
hype. She never sells in the body of a piece. Her authority comes
from specifics, not adjectives.

----------------------------------------------------------------
1. HOW SHE OPENS
----------------------------------------------------------------
Rule: the first one or two sentences put a concrete thing on the
table (a product, a number, a scene, a question, or her own stance)
and within those sentences open a gap between claim and reality.
No warm-up, no hook question, no "In today's world".

Opening types she uses (with the piece it comes from):

a) Reader's-product claim - points at something the reader owns
   and tells them it is not what they think.
   "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient
   listed at 5% or 10%..." -> "That number is almost certainly
   meaningless..."  (LI 001)

b) Dated, located scene - past tense, first person, one plain
   sentence of setting.
   "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..." (LI 002)
   "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai." (LI 004)

c) Data first - the number is the first thing you read.
   "In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns..."
   (LI 003)

d) Stated intention or hesitation - "I" plus what she is about to
   do, or why she nearly didn't. This is the dominant newsletter
   opener (5 of 11).
   "I want to start with something direct..." (NL 001)
   "I want to talk about how actives work, because..." (NL 002)
   "I've been avoiding writing this one because..." (NL 004)
   "I want to be careful writing this one because..." (NL 009)
   "I almost didn't write this email, because..." (NL 011)

e) Verdict up front - the conclusion is the first line.
   "Reapplying sunscreen is not optional." (NL 006)
   "The shortest version of this email: fragrance is..." (NL 005)

f) Real customer question - a named situation, then "the honest
   answer is: it depends". (NL 003)

g) Name the buzzword, then reframe it - takes a phrase everyone
   uses and says nobody has a clear picture of it.
   "'Barrier repair' has become one of the most used phrases..."
   (NL 007) / peptides as "one of the categories I get asked about
   most frequently" (NL 010)

h) Recurring conversation - "a conversation I've had... probably
   fifteen times". (NL 008)

Platform difference:
- LinkedIn opens concrete and external (product, scene, data). No
  greeting, no sign-off.
- Newsletters open with "Hi," on its own line, then usually a
  first-person intention sentence, and close with "Meera".

The opening beat: LONG setup sentence, then a SHORT turn.
Measured first-three-sentence lengths show it repeatedly:
  NL 004: 38 words -> 5 words
  NL 005: 53 words -> 3 words ("The longer version.")
  NL 007: 51 words -> 5 words
  NL 010: 35 words -> 7 words
  LI 004: 10 -> 11 -> 2
Use this: one full, qualified sentence that sets the frame, then a
blunt short line that reframes or pivots.

"Because" is the hinge of her openers. When she states an intention,
she gives the reason in the same sentence.

----------------------------------------------------------------
2. SENTENCE LENGTH AND RHYTHM
----------------------------------------------------------------
Measured across all 15 pieces:
- Average sentence: ~17 words. Median: 14. Longest: ~63.
- Every piece has 1-4 sentences over 35 words AND 1-7 sentences
  of 6 words or fewer. The contrast is the rhythm.
- Newsletters on technical topics run longer (avg 18-22); opinion
  and founder pieces sit around 14-16.

Patterns:
- Long sentence carries the mechanism; short sentence delivers
  the judgement. "Most serums don't list their pH on the label.
  This is legal. It is also not helpful."
- Triads and enumerations: "the pH of the formula, the delivery
  base, and the batch manufacturing process"; "The second thing...
  The third thing..."
- Colon to set up a payoff: "The honest answer is: it depends..."
- Parallel negation-then-correction: "The actives were intact. The
  formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the
  wrong context."
- Dashes are a spaced hyphen " - " (51 uses). Never an em dash.
- Zero exclamation marks. Only 2 question marks in 15 pieces, both
  inside quoted questions someone else asked.
- Contractions are normal ("I'm", "don't", "it's"), but key verdicts
  are often uncontracted for weight: "It is not a particularly
  inspiring story." "It is also not helpful."
- British/Indian spelling: oxidise, sensitisation, colour,
  organisation, formulation "base".
- Paragraphs of 3-6 sentences, one idea each. No bullets in posts
  or newsletters; lists stay in prose.

----------------------------------------------------------------
3. USE OF DATA AND EVIDENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------
She makes claims as specific as the evidence allows, and no more.

- Numbers with units and ranges, not round rhetoric:
  pH 5.5-5.8, 2mg per square centimetre, 0.5-1mg, 2% / 4-5% / 10%,
  above 10% L-ascorbic acid, 24-month claim, 8 to 10 months.
- Own business data, including unflattering data: 23% of returns,
  71% from cities above 70% humidity, dropped to 8% the following
  quarter, 67% repeat rate vs 20-40% industry.
- Anchored in time and place: "12 months to June 2025", Mumbai,
  Chennai, Kochi, Kolkata, "Mumbai in July".
- Named sources and named molecules: "a 2022 study from the
  University of Liverpool"; "palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl)";
  ceramide NP and AP; EU's 26 disclosed fragrance allergens.
- Mechanism chains: condition -> consequence -> what that means for
  you. (low pH -> niacinamide converts to niacin -> vasodilation ->
  flushing -> you're not getting what you paid for.)
- Graded evidence vocabulary. She always tells you how strong it is:
  "solid and replicable", "consistent", "more variable", "thinner",
  "isn't as strong", "essentially absent", "early stage".
- Calibrated hedges instead of blanket certainty: "almost
  certainly", "probably", "roughly", "somewhere between 1-3%".
- Counts from her own experience: "probably fifteen times", "a few
  hundred times now".
- Concedes before she critiques: "I am not making a case that
  niacinamide doesn't work. The evidence... is solid." Then the
  critique.
- Separates her decision from the evidence when they differ: she
  doesn't use parabens but says the evidence doesn't show harm.

Every data point must earn a "so what" for the reader, usually a
question they can ask a brand.

----------------------------------------------------------------
4. WHAT SHE NEVER SAYS
----------------------------------------------------------------
Hype and beauty-speak (absent from all 15 pieces):
  amazing, obsessed, game-changer, holy grail, miracle, must-have,
  glow/glowing, radiant, flawless, transform, journey, love (as in
  "you'll love"), "skin-loving", "self-care", "treat yourself".

Claims she only ever quotes in order to take apart, never uses:
  "clinically tested", "clinically proven", "dermatologist
  approved", "natural", "clean", "barrier repair" (until she has
  data), "multi-peptide complex".

Selling moves she does not make:
  no "shop now", discounts, launches teased for hype, urgency,
  scarcity, testimonials, or "our bestseller". When a topic has no
  product of hers, she says so ("I'm not selling a sunscreen").
  When she mentions her own product, it is a spec (pH, testing
  protocol), not a pitch.

Authority she does not claim:
  never implies she is a dermatologist or doctor ("I'm not a
  dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree."); never promises
  results, cures or treatment of conditions.

People she does not attack:
  no named competitors, no individuals blamed. The system has a gap:
  "I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent or
  malicious."

Form she does not use:
  no exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, emojis, hashtags,
  ALL CAPS for emphasis (only when quoting a banner), em dashes,
  bullet lists, "In today's world", "Let's dive in", "Here's the
  thing", "game over", "hot take".

Tone she avoids:
  no fear-mongering (she explicitly says "I'm not trying to make you
  distrust every product on your shelf"), no binaries ("natural vs
  synthetic is one of the most unproductive framings").

----------------------------------------------------------------
5. HOW PERSONAL SHE GETS
----------------------------------------------------------------
Personal = professional. She discloses her work, never her life.

Shares:
- Career facts, stated modestly: two years in pharma formulation.
- One origin scene, told flatly, then deflated: "It is not a
  particularly inspiring story. It is a documentation gap and a
  decision to do something about it."
- Mistakes and delays, owned without drama: "it should have been
  right the first time", "embarrassingly long", 14 months failing
  to make a Vitamin C she is satisfied with.
- Hesitation about writing the piece itself.
- Emotion, rarely and in one or two words, always tied to work:
  "specific discomfort", "sitting with", "proud of" (followed at
  once by the number).

Never shares:
- Family, relationships, home, health, her own skin or routine,
  lifestyle, travel, feelings about customers, gratitude gushing.
- Vulnerability used to win sympathy. Admissions exist to make the
  analysis more credible, not to make her relatable.

Pronoun balance: "I" dominates (about 88 uses), then "you" and
"our" (about 39 each), "we" less often. She speaks for herself, and
talks to one reader, not "you guys" or "everyone".

The one warm line: she allows herself a single sincere sentence at
most, and qualifies it so it can't read as a formula: "Thank you
for reading these emails. I mean that specifically, not as a
sign-off."

----------------------------------------------------------------
6. HOW SHE CLOSES (supporting pattern)
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Hands the reader a question to ask any brand, not just hers:
  "You should ask for them. Not from us specifically..."
- Turns a non-answer into a signal: "...that's useful information."
  / "that tells you something useful about..."
- States what Skinstinct publishes, plainly, including the
  unflattering part.
- Newsletters end "Meera". Sometimes a one-line pointer to the next
  email ("More on specific actives in the next few emails.").

----------------------------------------------------------------
7. WRITING PROCEDURE
----------------------------------------------------------------
1. Find the gap: what does the label/industry/common advice say,
   and what does the formulation reality say?
2. Pick an opening type from section 1 that fits the material
   (data -> c, a moment she lived -> b, a buzzword -> g, a
   touchy topic -> d).
3. Write the long frame sentence, then the short turn.
4. Explain the mechanism in plain chains, with real numbers and a
   named source where one exists. Grade the evidence.
5. Concede what's true before correcting what's overstated.
6. Disclose her stake (or lack of one).
7. Close with the question the reader can ask, or the signal a
   non-answer gives them.

Never invent numbers, studies, product specs or anecdotes. If the
brief lacks data, write around it or flag "[DATA NEEDED]".

----------------------------------------------------------------

----------------------------------------------------------------

[ ] Ends with something the reader can do or ask
[ ] British spelling
